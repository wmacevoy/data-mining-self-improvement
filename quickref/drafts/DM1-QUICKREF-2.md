# DM1 quick reference

One line per principle of DM1, a guide of 100 data-mining guidelines for humans and agents, condensed so a reader who will never open the chapters gets the move and its reason. It is read beside SE1's quick reference and assumes it: each DM1 principle either reframes an SE1 principle for data or is new, and a line here says what data work adds. These are guidelines, not law: they point, and you decide; where a line leaves a hole, that hole is a place to decide in. The ID finds the full text in `guide/` when a line is not enough.

## 1. Purpose

- **DM1/P1** Software asks whether the thing works; data asks whether the claim is true, and before that whether anyone would do anything differently if it were. The fifth constraint, and usually the honest one to move, is the question: one lot instead of all of them, the next two hours instead of the season. Narrowing it is the commonest form of success.
- **DM1/P2** Accuracy is a property of a model against a held-out sample; truth is a property of a claim about a world with selection bias, revised measurements, and people who behave differently once the model is watching. A forecast that scores well on last season says nothing about the first season with a concert venue next door, and nothing in the score will say so. A claim carries its interval, its population, and when it stops holding.
- **DM1/P3** The person in the row did not agree to the question, will never see the output, and meets only its consequences. `groupby("lot")` and `groupby("plate")` look the same in code; the second is about people. Minimization, aggregation, and recourse reduce their discomfort; when someone stops wanting to re-run the pipeline, that is information about the pipeline.
- **DM1/P4** A recorded seed retires "the result was luck"; a holdout touched once retires "I fit the thing I was measuring with"; a row-count assertion retires "I silently tripled my dataset." A leaking split, 99% on a 99%-negative class, and a reproducibility badge on a pipeline nobody has re-run cold look exactly the same and retire nothing. A wrong bridge falls down; a wrong number just gets used.
- **DM1/P5** The deliverable is the claim plus someone else's ability to regenerate it from raw, argue with it, and update it next quarter. A notebook records cells, not the order you ran them in, and the order is the program. Keep notebooks for looking and the method in code a command can run.

## 2. Unknowing

- **DM1/U1** Mining is discovering which K each column belongs to, in the right order and at the right price. K1 converts by looking: profile, plot, ask who collected it. K2 cannot be searched for, only met sooner: run end to end on real data early, show an expert, score on a period you did not develop on. K2 here is a certainty, and it arrives as a sentence: "that column means something different before 2024."
- **DM1/U2** Software that is wrong tends to stop; analysis that is wrong continues and produces a chart. A join that duplicates rows returns a DataFrame; a `dropna()` that removes exactly the interesting cases returns a smaller, cleaner, misleading table; a partition half-written when the run died reads as current. Give each a way to say wrong: assert the row count across every join, overwrite rather than append, and treat what you find after a gap as torn until a run from raw agrees.
- **DM1/U3** Leakage is any path by which the answer reaches the model in training that will not be there at prediction time: a split with the same lot and night on both sides, an "arrival time" that is really the upload time. It does not feel like a mistake: the metric improves and cross-validation agrees, because everything you would use to catch an error is the thing being fooled. A result too good for the problem is a bug report until one prediction is traced back to rows that existed at the prediction point.
- **DM1/U4** Rows are independent; the sample is the population; the label is the truth; missing is missing at random; a day is twenty-four hours. Every entry is false and every entry is in use. Choose each on purpose and write what breaks when it comes due: independence fails and the intervals are too narrow; stationarity fails and the backtest was fiction. The distance between a chosen K4 and K3 is one written sentence.
- **DM1/U5** "Two days to clean this dataset" is a K0 statement about K2 work. Cleaning and exploration get a time box ending on a fence; modeling from a known-good feature table, with a fixed metric and split, gets an estimate. "I'll know by Thursday whether the gate data is usable" is honest; "the model will be done Thursday" hides a cleaning box inside a modeling estimate.
- **DM1/U6** A classifier shown something unlike anything it trained on does not raise; it returns a class, often with high confidence, and a checkpoint from an interrupted retrain answers as confidently as the finished model. Cheapest first: refuse or flag inputs outside the training envelope; calibrate, so 0.8 means right about 80% of the time; abstain below a confidence and count how often. A rising abstention rate is a drift alarm that needs no labels.
- **DM1/U7** A mean, a correlation, or an accuracy drops things without knowing what it dropped; Anscombe's four datasets share all three and look nothing alike. Check from outside: a plot, ten rows at random, then the rows that most disagree with the number. For a document the text layer is a summary of the page; look at the page. Left as a habit this is the step the deadline skips, so have the command that makes the number print the plot and the ten worst rows every run.
- **DM1/U8** "Will I find a space in lot C?" is a question about one case; the data holds how often lot C was full on nights like this one, and whether this driver finds a space depends on things not in the rows. A probability for one outcome describes a reference class, the rows the model cannot tell apart from yours. Say it that way, and name the class. Small groups get pooled toward the majority, so for a minority the gap is systematic in one direction: that gap is bias, and it is found only by looking for it.
- **DM1/U9** Time with no agenda finds the K2 nobody asked about, and data work needs it more and can afford it less, because every look at the outcome that changes what you try next spends evidence. So point the whitespace at the inputs: the raw rows, the extremes, the datasheet, a column's distribution over time. A choice made with the outcome out of view does not select for a good score. A look at the outcome is a look, and goes in the ledger.
- **DM1/U10** An absence is the cheapest K3 there is: it takes no evidence to report and looks like diligence, and a search that could not have found the thing returns the same answer as one that found nothing. Every "none found" says how it was looked for, and the search has been seen to find a planted case. Before reporting something missing across many cases, open one by hand.
- **DM1/U11** An AUC of 0.91 is K0. That this model is good enough to change what the gate supervisor does on Saturday is not, and no further analysis settles it; say "the model passes" and the question stops being asked. Convert what converts into a bar; mark the rest *(judged)* and name who judges it, on what. A verdict belongs to one version: a retrain or a moved threshold makes it stale, unless the judge approved a retrain policy in advance. An agent records a verdict and never gives one.

## 3. Feedback

- **DM1/F1** Re-running a transform or swapping a model class is free; a training run, a labeling campaign, or an hour of a domain expert is not; looking at the holdout or publishing a number someone acts on can be done once. All three live in the same afternoon. The one-way decisions mostly come in the first week, while they still feel cheap: the question, the unit, the outcome, the split, the labeling scheme.
- **DM1/F2** The toolkit, in data: pure functions over frames, a tiny fixture with answers worked out by hand, seeds fixed and recorded with the result, one command from raw to every reported number, assertions on what joins and filters break, caches that record when and from where. Lowering the cost of checking a result multiplies against every future question. If a result is hard to check, that is information about the analysis, not about checking.
- **DM1/F3** Loops by cost: look at the data, assert in the pipeline, unit-test a transform, run the fixture end to end, compare with the baseline, spend the holdout, have a person review the claim, show a domain expert the output, use it prospectively. No test suite detects "we measured the wrong thing," and no metric detects "these rows do not contain the people we care about." Only someone who knows the domain does.
- **DM1/F4** In software, trying forty implementations and keeping the one that passes leaves no residue. In data, try forty features or subgroups and keep the best, and what you kept was selected for looking good, some of it noise: a tournament crowned its luckiest contestant. A result is real to the degree it would have survived being the only thing tried. Your hyperparameter search is an adversary you hired; given enough freedom it finds your leakage and hands it back as a win.
- **DM1/F5** You cannot un-see the test set. Each time you evaluate on it and change something in response, some of its information moves into the model through your judgment, leaving no trace in git; twenty times and you have a validation set wearing a test set's name. Split it off first, keep it where casual code cannot reach it, develop on validation, spend it at a decision, and when it is spent, say so and get a fresh one.
- **DM1/F6** "We tried one model" and "we tried sixty" are different claims about the same score, and the second is almost never reported. Count mechanically: a ledger line the code appends every time anything is scored, and a plan written before the outcome is examined, so a departure is visible. Then say the number: "best of 23 on validation; one look at the holdout."
- **DM1/F7** A number without an interval is not more certain, only less honest; 0.84 against 0.82 is a coin flip on a 1,200-row test set. Three spreads, all routinely ignored: the sample, the seed, and the split. Train five times with five seeds; a gain smaller than the seed-to-seed spread is not a gain.
- **DM1/F8** As a sample grows, variance shrinks and bias does not: a large biased sample converges confidently on the wrong number, with the error bar tight around a value that was never right. Sensors drop off the network most when the lot is busiest, so five years of readings describe the quiet hours very precisely. "We have millions of rows" does not answer "who is missing from them?"
- **DM1/F9** The optimizer gaming your metric is usually not a person but the training loop, the search, or an agent re-running until the score goes up, none of them knowing what the metric was a proxy for. Aim at accuracy on an imbalanced class and get the majority class; aim at a rubric and get the rubric. Report a second metric the search was not told about, and look at individual predictions. A limit is a target too: cap a summary's length and the summaries crowd under the cap; what changes that is comparing the drafts by script, not telling the writer.
- **DM1/F10** A citation check holds what a summary says to its source; it cannot see what the summary stopped saying. Every quotation can be exact and the most important sentence gone: fidelity is precision, and nothing about it measures recall. Keep the drafts and diff them against the final by script; have a second reader, blind to the first, read a sample whole and count what each missed. When a limit on length and the evidence conflict, the limit gives way.

## 4. Requirements

- **DM1/R1** The client asks for "patterns in the parking data"; underneath is a decision someone makes badly today: two hours before an event, which lots to open and which signs to light. Find that decision first, then ask what you would do differently if the answer came out the other way. If nothing, the analysis is a hobby, and should be budgeted as one.
- **DM1/R2** One row is one what: a reading, a lot-hour, a lot-event? That decides what counts as independent and how the split is grouped. The outcome is not "full" but something you could write as a query. The prediction point is the moment the prediction is made, two hours before gates open, and every feature must be knowable then. Each costs a re-analysis to change later, so fix all three before anything else.
- **DM1/R3** "A good model" cannot be rejected. "Predicts when each lot fills to within fifteen minutes for 80% of events, against a baseline of *same as last comparable event* that manages 55%, on the season we have not looked at" can, and getting it rejected in week one is the point. A bar set after the score is set to the score. The bar can say no; it cannot say the result is worth acting on, so write who decides that.
- **DM1/R4** A classifier has at least two prices and they are almost never equal: predicted full and wasn't sends drivers to a far lot past empty spaces; predicted space and was full queues cars at a closed gate. Get the ratio from the client, even as "one queue is worse than ten wasted walks," and choose the threshold from it. The threshold is a business decision, not a hyperparameter; accuracy prices both errors the same.
- **DM1/R5** Whatever pandas does with a missing value becomes the method, chosen by nobody. Absent: ask why before how to fill; a sensor that goes unreachable at peak is the peak, and `dropna()` deletes the event. Late: the reading revised next month. Wrong: `-999`, a changed unit; a plot finds most, which is why the survivors are the ones nobody plotted. Lying: a zero meaning "no reading" among zeros meaning "empty," passing every schema check; only provenance finds it.
- **DM1/R6** Train on data as it looks now, score on the past, and you have used the future. Final attendance sits in the same row as ticket sales and is not known until the event ends, with nothing in the file to say so. For every column, write down when its value became available to someone who could act on it, and build features with joins that respect that.
- **DM1/R7** A label was applied by someone, under some instructions, at some time, for a purpose that may not be yours. "Lot full" may mean an attendant radioed it in, which depends on who was working. Measure how often two labelers disagree on a sample: the model is bounded by that rate. A labeling scheme is a requirement; version it like one.
- **DM1/R8** Scale here is rows, and especially events: five seasons of readings may be eighty events, and the rare unit is the one that limits you. Then labels, at what cost per label and by when; compute per run, and how many runs the plan assumes; hours of the person who knows the domain, often the scarcest of all; and how stale the model may get before someone must retrain it.
- **DM1/R9** When rows are people, three questions become mandatory: who is missing and what does the model do to them; is the label a proxy for what you care about or for how people were treated; can the person scored contest the score. The safest record is the one never kept; the second safest is the aggregate instead of the row. "We removed the names" is a sentence about names: ZIP, birth date, and sex identify most of the population. A request to add license-plate reads is one join in code and a new kind of system in the requirements.
- **DM1/R10** Bias is a systematic gap between what the data estimates and what is true for the people or cases it is used on: a property of the pipeline, either measured or present unmeasured. It enters as history the labels repeat, as who was easy to measure, as a label recorded differently per group, as small groups pooled toward large. The requirements say which groups matter, which gap is unacceptable, and measured how; when base rates differ, a score cannot be both equally calibrated and equally wrong for every group, and choosing belongs to the client with the affected people represented. A forecast late for the small lots is biased against the small lots.
- **DM1/R11** A model's non-goals are populations, conditions, and uses: lots that did not exist in the training seasons, events larger than any seen, use as a staffing tool when it was built for signage. In each the model still returns an answer. Written down, they are the model card's out-of-scope section and the envelope checks at its input; unwritten, they are discovered by whoever uses it for them.
- **DM1/R12** Say which kind of claim this is before anything else, because that decides what it can be scored against. Predicted: an outcome that arrives later. Described: a held-out period. Explained: an experiment or a stated causal design. Extracted: the document itself, which a person can check at the page, though the check cannot see what was left out. Where a principle does not bind your kind, say so rather than fill its template with something invented.

## 5. Design

- **DM1/D1** In a data system the code is small and the state is everything else: raw pulls, clean tables, feature tables, splits, trained weights, vector indexes. It persists (last month's code built this month's table), it is shared ("the clean table" means two versions), it ages, and it changes while observed. A trained model is state, not code: it cannot be read, only re-derived or tested, and a change to it reaches every prediction at once.
- **DM1/D2** A trained model is a cache of the relationship between inputs and outcome as of its training window; drift is cache staleness with a statistical name. A local copy of an API pull with no fetch time is a time machine: green this morning, out of contact with reality for a month. A vector index is a cache of the corpus under one embedding model. For each, put the age in the data: `fetched_at`, `trained_through`.
- **DM1/D3** Every row in a training set has its own "now," and a join on today's tables gives every historical row the benefit of every later correction: leakage by construction. Join as of the prediction point: for each row, the latest value whose available-at time is before it. That needs two times per value, when it was true and when it became known; most sources store only the first, so write down the lag you are assuming.
- **DM1/D4** A pipeline gets re-run after a crash, after a fix, by someone not sure it finished. A stage that appends doubles its output on the second run and nothing downstream says so; a count of events just looks busier. A stage that overwrites its own partition, this day, this lot, this run, can run any number of times. Fetch incrementally from the last recorded high-water mark and de-duplicate on a key.
- **DM1/D5** Timestamps are data, not formatting: UTC with explicit offsets inside, local time at the edges, once. Then decide what a day is, because a daily aggregate over UTC splits an evening event across two days and disagrees with the local chart beside it. Two local days a year are 23 and 25 hours long; a naive series shows a phantom gap in spring and a negative step in fall. Test both boundaries.
- **DM1/D6** Raw stays exactly as it arrived, with where and when it came from; everything else, down to the number in the slide, is derived by code and can be deleted and rebuilt with one command. A model is a function of data version, code version, seed, and environment: record all four, or the first time it misbehaves you cannot tell whether the data, the code, or the luck changed. Where raw is not yours and may change, hash each file on arrival; that is detection, which is what you can have.
- **DM1/D7** A transform is a function from frames to a frame: no fetching, no file paths, no clock, no unseeded randomness inside. A notebook is a fine place to call those functions and look at what comes back, and a poor place to define them: hidden state and out-of-order cells make its real program unknowable. When a cell is worth keeping, move it into a module and test it with a five-row frame typed by hand.
- **DM1/D8** For a model, the measure of accidental complexity is the baseline. An ensemble with 400 features that beats *same as last comparable event* by two minutes, inside the seed-to-seed spread, bought nothing and must still be carried, monitored, explained, and retrained. Prefer the simplest model whose margin survives the interval; every feature is a dependency on a source that can go absent, late, wrong, or lying.
- **DM1/D9** One named quantity, one definition. The expensive violation is train/serve skew: a feature computed one way in training and another where predictions are served, a different window or a different handling of missing values, so the model is trained on one thing and fed another, and no offline metric can see it. One function, imported by both; the same for the outcome, the metric, and "day." The mission here is the decision the work serves, and the fundamental shape is a few guidelines like these, written at the top of the question.
- **DM1/D10** A model cannot be read inside at all, so its contract carries more weight: the inputs and their units, the population and period it was trained on, its metrics by slice with intervals, what it does outside its envelope, and what it is not for. That is a model card. A dataset gets the same: a datasheet.
- **DM1/D11** Once a team has tracked a number for a year, its definition is an interface with dependents: comparisons, targets, decisions. You may change it; you may not change it silently. Name the new one, run both for a while, and say when the old one stops. A published number has an installed base of at least one decision.
- **DM1/D12** Every deployed model carries a weekly cost: monitoring, retraining, its feature pipeline, the person who answers for it. The model code is the small box. It is also perishable, and nothing announces the expiry: someone has to own the question of whether last year's model is still true, with a date on which they will ask it.
- **DM1/D13** A number with no lineage is a rumor. A value: measured, imputed, or defaulted, and once all three share a column they are the same value forever, so keep a flag. A dataset: collected by whom, of whom, and what was excluded before it reached you, the exclusions being invisible in the file and usually the most important thing in it. A label: by whom, under which instructions. A result: data version, commit, seed, environment, the layer people skip, and why a number that reproduced in September stops reproducing in March.

## 6. Testing

- **DM1/T1** The test that would pass when it works is the evaluation harness: the split, the metric, the bar, the report. Build it before any model exists, and enter the dumbest contestant first: the mean, the majority class, *same as the last comparable event*. The harness's bugs are found while the stakes are low, every later model has a number to beat, and sometimes the baseline wins, which is a finding and a cheap one.
- **DM1/T2** A reliable pipeline checks itself on every run, on real data: row counts across every join and filter, key uniqueness, a monotone gap-checked time index, units and plausible ranges where data enters, the fraction missing compared with the last run. Each turns a returned value back into an exception. A failed assertion that stops a run is a gift; the same problem as a plausible number is a liability.
- **DM1/T3** Data tests love to assert shape: the output is non-empty, the column exists, the row count survived. Those pass on garbage. A fixture too small never reaches the logic: one day fed to a daily aggregate is one row, a rolling z-score over it is `NaN`, and input length equals output length compares 1 to 1. Assert values worked out by hand, and treat every instrument that judges a result, a metric script, a labeler, a model used as a judge, the same way: see it disagree with a bad answer first.
- **DM1/T4** The untrusted loop here is a metric that moves between runs with nothing changed: unseeded randomness, nondeterministic data order, a source that returns a different revision. After the third unexplained wobble, a real regression of the same size gets the same shrug. Make every run reproduce its number, or report the spread it is expected to have, so a move outside it means something.
- **DM1/T5** A seed is necessary and not sufficient: GPU kernels, thread scheduling, and the order of floating-point sums change results across machines. Demand bit-for-bit equality everywhere and you get flaky tests; demand nothing and you get untestable claims. Decide per claim: transforms and splits are identical; model outputs and metrics agree within a tolerance taken from the observed spread, not from what makes the test pass; seed-dependent results are tested as a mean and spread over several seeds.
- **DM1/T6** A synthetic dataset is a mock of reality, and a clean one tests only the happy path. Build the fixture from the ugliest things seen in the real data: a sentinel, a duplicate key, a sensor that went unreachable at the peak, a value revised after the fact, both daylight-saving boundaries, a category never seen in training. Small enough to work the answers out by hand; committed with its expected output. It is the cheapest test in the repository.
- **DM1/T7** A code review shows what changed in the code, not what changed in the output, and in a data system the output is the change that matters. "Shouldn't change anything" is a hypothesis, testable in seconds against the fixture. Commit a golden output, regenerate it with every change, and review the diff: an unexpected one is a finding, an expected one is documented in the same commit.
- **DM1/T8** State the rule a model must obey for every input and let the machine generate the cases. Invariance: renaming a lot does not change its forecast. Direction: more tickets sold must not predict more free spaces. Minimum functionality: cases any working model gets right. Each runs in seconds and catches what an aggregate score hides.
- **DM1/T9** An overall score is an average over slices, and an average can be fine while one slice is broken: the small lots, the concerts, the people under-represented in the data. Choose the slices with the question, before results, so they are not chosen to flatter, and report each with its *n* and interval. A slice is a group, and its error is the treatment that group receives; this is where bias becomes something to check rather than assert.
- **DM1/T10** When someone else owns correctness, a reference library, the original runtime of a model you exported, the paper whose number you reimplemented, their implementation is the judge. Run yours and theirs on the same input and compare within a tolerance; explain any gap.
- **DM1/T11** Clusters, association rules, and anomaly scores have no answer key, which makes them the easiest results to believe and the hardest to check. Re-run on a different month: clusters that rearrange themselves were drawn, not found. Vary the seed, *k*, the distance, the scaling: a pattern that survives one setting is a property of the setting. Show a domain expert five members of each cluster, not the centroids, and ask whether they belong together.
- **DM1/T12** The dumb baseline for a search system is not another search; it is a person, or a model, reading the whole document. Run that on a sample before trusting any retrieval, and report what the search missed. The same fixed searches for every case are fair and can miss the same thing in every case: equal treatment is not recall. And keep the test questions away from whoever tunes the search, or the test has become a training set.
- **DM1/T13** A run is one data point for one question, which makes it a test of a claim, so say before it what result would count against the claim; a run with nothing at stake is a look that still counts and tests nothing. Start small, one lot, one configuration, and let a small run clear the next size, though it cannot stand in for it. Every run gets its line in the ledger, whatever it cost; what is attached to the line scales with what it would cost to run again, up to everything you may keep for a holdout look, since that record is all there will ever be. A limit on keeping data about people comes first.

## 7. Operating

- **DM1/O1** The result, not only the build, depends on the environment: a different BLAS, a GPU instead of a CPU, a minor version with a changed default, and a metric moves in the third digit or the second. Every trained model and reported number carries the resolved package list, the platform, and the accelerator beside it. Saving the package manager's list into the run directory costs nothing and answers "what changed?" months later.
- **DM1/O2** CI runs the command you run, and for a data repository that command goes from raw to report on the committed fixture: every stage, the assertions, the property tests, the golden-output diff, in minutes. CI does not touch the holdout or the full data: it tests that the method runs and is right on known cases; whether the method is good is a decision, spent on purpose. A repository whose data may not leave still has one script every change passes through, a gate you can skip.
- **DM1/O3** A model's characteristic failure is to keep answering, confidently and wrongly, and the signal you want, accuracy, needs the true outcome, which arrives tonight, in years, or never. Monitor what you can see now: each feature's distribution against training, the rate of out-of-envelope inputs, the distribution of predictions, the abstention rate, the freshness of each source. Then, as labels arrive, realized error and calibration by slice.
- **DM1/O4** "Drift" is three failures. Covariate shift, the inputs change: a new ticketing system; visible by comparing distributions. Prior shift, the outcome gets more common: a winning season; partly visible in the prediction rate. Concept shift, the relationship itself changes: a new transit line, same tickets, fewer cars; inputs and predictions look normal and only realized error shows it. The third is why label latency belongs in the requirements.
- **DM1/O5** When the input is missing or out of envelope, the service is down, or monitoring says drift, something must still answer, and it should be simple enough to trust without monitoring: the baseline, a rule the operators already use, or "no forecast" shown honestly. Decide it with the client, write it in the requirements, and test the switch. A model with no fallback turns every upstream outage into a confident wrong answer.
- **DM1/O6** Before a new model decides anything, run it in shadow: live inputs, logged predictions, the old model still deciding. Compare them on live data, including the slices, then switch in a way that can be undone, and keep the old model loadable. A scheduled retrain is a deploy though no code changed: new data, new weights, new behavior, so it gets the fixture, the properties, and the comparison before it serves.
- **DM1/O7** A feature table and a training set are interfaces to every model trained on them; give each a version, a content hash or a dated snapshot, recorded with every model. Changing a feature's definition is a migration: add the new column beside the old, backfill, retrain and compare, switch readers, then remove. Overwriting a feature in place silently changes the meaning of every model that reads it, including the one in production.
- **DM1/O8** A data system has more copies than its owners count: every cache, every intermediate file, every extract on a laptop, every notebook output cell, every prompt that pasted rows into a hosted model, an agent's transcripts, and the old versions inside a store where a deleted row stays until purged. Each is the data, with weaker protection. A model can disclose its training data and anyone who can write to the inputs can steer it, so treat training data as outside the trust boundary and a model trained on personal data as personal data.
- **DM1/O9** No step of a pipeline is unbiased by default, and a model that passed an audit at training time can fail it later as the population drifts from the one it learned. Before deploy, compute the per-group measures the requirements chose, on the holdout, with *n* and intervals, then try to make it look worse: search for the group it does worst on, and since that search is a search, confirm what it finds on fresh data. After deploy, watch the same gaps as labels arrive. A gap is a finding whose options go to the client; removing the sensitive column rarely fixes it, because other columns stand in for it.
- **DM1/O10** A pipeline developed on a sample meets three problems only at full size: the frame that no longer fits in memory, the key that was unique in the sample and is not across all seasons, and the stage that took seconds now taking hours, so nobody re-runs it. Run the whole thing at real size early, once, timed, and keep the time in the repository guide. The slow stage is rarely the model.
- **DM1/O11** You can roll back a model; you cannot roll back the data it generated or the behavior it taught. The app shows drivers which lots will fill, so they go elsewhere, so those lots fill later, so next season's data says the forecast was wrong. Ship it like the one-way door it is: record what the model decided beside what happened, keep a small random slice that does not follow the model, and say in the model card what the model changes.

## 8. Work

- **DM1/W1** CRISP-DM is the six steps under other names: business and data understanding are requirements, evaluation is test, and monitoring, usually left out, is maintain. It draws one arrow the six steps only imply, from evaluation back to the question, and in data that arrow is the common case: evaluation regularly shows the outcome was the wrong definition or the horizon was wrong. The right next step is to rewrite the question, not tune the model.
- **DM1/W2** The expensive decisions in data work all arrive early, when nothing is built and they feel cheap. A model class or a feature costs a re-run: loop fast. The outcome, the unit, and the prediction point cost every feature, label, and result: write them, read them to the client, get an objection. The split costs every reported number: have someone try to break it. The labels cost the budget that bought them: pilot on fifty rows and measure agreement. Most arguments run hours on the model class and minutes on the outcome.
- **DM1/W3** Clean all the data, then engineer all the features, then model, then report: the horizontal plan puts the question's first contact with the data in the last week. On day one, fetch one lot, compute one feature, score the baseline on a proper split, and produce the actual chart the client will look at. Then thicken. The column that means something else and the missing peak are found while there is time to respond.
- **DM1/W4** Exploration is the data form of the spike, and it falls down the waterfall the same way: one more plot, one more join, no claim at the end. Give each exploration and cleaning pass a time box and end it on a fence, something proved: a baseline scored on the real split, a datasheet entry written, a plot an expert objected to, a cold run from raw with exit zero, a null written down. A status meeting is not a fence; neither is a notebook cell that ran.
- **DM1/W5** The deadline pathology here is the pressure to find something: one more peek at the holdout, one more subgroup where the effect is significant, the null quietly not mentioned. It never feels like cheating; it feels like trying harder. Change the terrain: make raw to report one command, write the plan and the bar first so a late change shows as a departure, let the ledger count so the forty-first try is visibly the forty-first, and report nulls when they happen.
- **DM1/W6** The working set adds the question, the analysis plan, and the ledger. The cheapest documentation in data work, and the one nobody writes, is what did not work: without it the next person, often you, re-runs the same dead end, and the count of things tried is lost. A dead end is a finding: what it cost, what would have found it sooner, and what it changed: an assertion in the pipeline, a note in the datasheet, a line in the plan, or why nothing.
- **DM1/W7** For a claim, done is the number with its interval, its slices, and the count of things tried; the command that regenerates it from raw, run by someone other than the author, producing the same number within tolerance; the datasheet and model card updated; the K4s read to the client; a reviewer who read the joins, filters, splits, and fills, not only the model; a verdict on this version for whatever is *(judged)*. For an extracted claim, a script re-finds each quotation on its page, which checks words, not omissions.

## 9. People

- **DM1/H1** The domain expert is usually the only loop that catches the most expensive errors: the sensor being replaced that week, the column whose meaning changed, the label everybody knows is unreliable on Sundays. They cannot see those in a metric. Show them a plot over time with anomalies marked, ten raw rows including the strangest five, the model's five worst predictions, five members of each cluster. Then ask what surprises them: their surprise is your K2, found cheaply.
- **DM1/H2** Most K4s are statistical sentences the client cannot evaluate as written. Translate each into their world and read it aloud. "Missing at random" becomes "we're assuming a sensor that goes quiet is no more likely to be at a busy lot. True?" "Stationarity" becomes "we're assuming next season's crowds behave like the last three." The answer is often "no, because…", and that sentence is worth more than any model trained before hearing it.
- **DM1/H3** "The effect isn't there" in week two is a finding, and a cheap one: the client can stop paying for the question or change it. The same sentence in week eleven is a crisis, and that pressure is what turns an honest null into a tortured positive. Say it in the tone you would use for a win; a null announced apologetically teaches the team that nulls are failures, and the next one is found later, or not at all.
- **DM1/H4** The contract between two people here is a definition: what counts as an event, what "occupied" means, which rows are excluded. When two people each clean "the" dataset, each decides those privately, and a third version appears when someone merges their work. Give each definition one owner and one implementation, each cleaned dataset one producing command, and let everyone else import it. A meeting that keeps recurring about why two dashboards disagree is a missing contract.
- **DM1/H5** The team's shape becomes the conclusions' shape: whoever owns the pipeline owns the definitions, the definitions decide the results, and the group that defines "full" decides how often lots are full. If that is going to happen anyway, choose it: put definitions where the people they affect can read and contest them, and do not let the team that is measured own the measure alone.
- **DM1/H6** "AUC 0.83" means nothing to the person deciding which lots to open. Translate into the decision's own terms with the uncertainty attached: "for four events in five, lot C fills within fifteen minutes of the forecast; for the fifth it can be off by forty." Frequencies are understood more reliably than probabilities, and they say honestly what the number is about: a reference class, not the one case the client is asking about. Say what the model cannot tell them in the same breath.
- **DM1/H7** A model demo's temptation is to pick the examples that work. Then the client sees a model that does not exist, and their objection, the most valuable output of the meeting, never comes. Choose the examples beforehand and write them down: some at random, some typical, and the worst ones the model gets wrong. Show the failures as clearly as the successes and ask which failures matter; that answer is a requirement.
- **DM1/H8** Discomfort about a number has a short list of shapes, each worth more said than kept. "This is too good" is the first sign of leakage. "I keep wanting to look at the holdout" is a look about to be spent. "I don't trust the Sunday labels" is a K4 about to become K3. "The effect went away when I changed the window" is a null being negotiated with. So the stand-up asks a fourth thing: what about the current number do you not believe? A disbelief nobody can name yet goes in FINDINGS as a question.

## 10. Agents

- **DM1/A1** An agent can write the pipeline, train a dozen models, and draft the report in an afternoon. None of that made the question better posed, the provenance clearer, the labels more reliable, or the holdout larger. Everything scarce has moved to the second half of the work: the question, the provenance, the checks, the review, and the ownership of the claim.
- **DM1/A2** An agent is a K3 factory, and data work is the worst place to install one, because here the wrongness does not raise: it merges on the wrong key, silently triples the rows, reports an improved metric, and summarizes it fluently. A run is not enough either; "it ran and the AUC went up" is the shape of leakage. Require what a run cannot fake: row counts before and after each join, a plot, one prediction traced back to its rows. And a check the agent is told to run is not a check that runs: put it in the pipeline and CI, and make the holdout unreachable rather than forbidden.
- **DM1/A3** Hand an agent the search and it searches at machine speed: sixty feature sets, forty configurations, a dozen ways to treat the outliers, and it reports the one that won, with the other fifty-nine paths in a transcript nobody reads. Hand it the ledger too: every configuration it scores is appended by the code, not by its summary, and the count is reported with the result.
- **DM1/A4** An agent in a loop with a score will optimize the score. If the holdout is readable it will eventually be read, not out of malice but because evaluating on it is an obvious way to "check the work," and every check it reacts to spends it. Make it mechanical, not a matter of instruction: outside the agent's working directory, or behind a permission it does not have. The plainest answer is a person who keeps it on paper; a shell cannot read paper.
- **DM1/A5** What agents do best and humans skip most is exactly what multiplies in data work: the hand-computed fixture, the row-count and uniqueness assertions around every join, the property tests, the golden output, the datasheet's first draft. More models do not multiply. More checks do.
- **DM1/A6** Spend review where K3 lives: the joins, filters, splits, fills, and time conversions, and the definition of the outcome. Model code is library calls and rarely wrong in an interesting way; a wrong `how=` in a merge or a `fillna(0)` where zero means something is one line and changes every number. Ask for the diff in the numbers as well as the diff in the code, and read the data handling line by line.
- **DM1/A7** Delegation is not abdication. If you cannot say which rows, which definition, which split, and how many tries produced the number, you cannot defend it, update it next quarter, or notice when it goes wrong. An analysis you cannot explain is a liability, whatever its metric.
- **DM1/A8** Pasting rows into a hosted model, attaching a dataset to a chat, or letting an agent read a table discloses that data to the provider, under their terms and retention, whatever your purpose. For data about people that can be a use it was not collected for. Decide before the first session what the agent may see, usually the schema and a synthetic sample: agents write good code from a fixture and rarely need the real rows.
- **DM1/A9** A language model that labels, extracts, embeds, or judges is a model, and four things need restating. The prompt and the model version are the model: an edit or a provider's update is a retrain. Its output is a distribution: score several samples and report the spread, and see a judge model disagree with a wrong answer before trusting its agreement. Its misses do not show in its citations: two independent reads of a sample, each blind to the other, with what each missed counted. And its test set may be in its training: evaluate on questions written after the cutoff, or held privately.
- **DM1/A10** A synthetic document can test the code and cannot summarize the real one, so when reading the rows is the task, the agent reads them, and what then has to be true is written before the first session: who decided and what the people in the data were told; which service sees it, under what terms; every copy, including the agent's own transcripts, tool output, and scratch files; that text in a document is evidence, never an instruction; and when it all gets removed, by a procedure that has been tried.
